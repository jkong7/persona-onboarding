# Durable state lets conversational onboarding survive hangups

The candidate should build one conversational agent whose memory lives on the server, not in the call: every captured field is written through a tool call the moment it is heard, voice and text read and write the same record, and a hangup, refresh or channel switch simply reattaches a new session to that record and continues from the first missing item. On top of that core, the evidence supports four concrete choices: run the call over a browser WebRTC speech-to-speech session (OpenAI Realtime is the researchers' first pick, at an estimated $55 to $90 for 100 three-minute calls), let the user "graduate" as soon as a help topic exists, connect Gmail through a click-triggered popup requesting only `gmail.readonly` with the evaluator pre-listed as a test user, and ship a clearly labelled sample inbox for anyone who cannot or will not connect a real account. These choices matter because the brief names hangups and rule-breaking users as the grading criteria, and because Persona's own marketing promises "No prompts, no settings, no onboarding," which makes any form-like sequence off-brand. The important caveat is that Persona's live onboarding was not observed during this research; its actual sequence, wording and Gmail mechanics are unknown, and the brief itself is the best available description. Much of the design guidance below is researcher inference built on framework documentation and vendor material, and it is labelled as such. No retrieved source shows that naming an assistant improves retention, that voice onboarding outperforms text, or that conversational collection beats a short form at four fields, so the candidate should justify the design as a demonstration of the product and not with completion-rate claims.

## Rivals converge on number, OAuth, talk, and none documents a voice onboarding call

Every competitor flow described here comes from third-party write-ups, vendor pages or press; no product was used first-hand. With that limit, the text-native assistants share a short skeleton. Poke's current flow is sign up with name and phone number, pick a messaging platform, connect Gmail or Outlook, and a text thread opens, with the email link described as "what unlocks most of the assistant's usefulness" ([Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/poke-review/), [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/08/poke-makes-ai-agents-as-easy-as-sending-a-text/)). One Lindy reviewer went phone number, Gmail, Calendar, Notion and reported "Setup took me about two minutes" followed by a proactive 7 AM briefing within the first day ([Mejba review](https://www.mejba.me/blog/lindy-ai-executive-assistant-review)). Martin asks for a phone number and email, then connects calendar, inbox, contacts and Slack ([Martin docs](https://docs.trymartin.com/introduction)). Cora analyses email history at sign-up to learn reply patterns and style ([Cora](https://cora.computer/)), and Howie's onboarding is praised in a vendor-selected testimonial for inferring defaults and letting the user adjust them ([howie.com](https://howie.com/)). The researcher's reading is that step count is already near its floor, so differentiation sits in the first few agent messages after the connection, not in removing steps.

What users like clusters on three things: living inside an existing message thread, fast setup, and proactive first value. Lindy holds 4.9 out of 5 on G2 across 171 reviews with setup ease the most-mentioned positive, a figure relayed by a competitor blog ([Carly blog](https://www.usecarly.com/blog/lindy-ai-review/)). A Martin reviewer wrote, "I love how proactive it is—like getting a daily briefing without asking" ([Product Hunt](https://www.producthunt.com/products/martin/reviews)). Poke's launch-era onboarding, in which the agent used the writer's own email context to roast her and made her argue for admission, hooked one writer precisely because it was unlike typical AI validation ([Tanisha Srivatsa](https://tanishasrivatsa.substack.com/p/i-spent-30-minutes-arguing-with-a)).

What users dislike is more instructive. **Poke's "bouncer" was cited as both its best and worst feature**: "Aggressive onboarding 'bouncer' can be off-putting for some," with one commenter writing "I feel like the bouncer hates me" ([Product Hunt](https://www.producthunt.com/p/poke-by-interaction-co/a-week-with-poke-review-a-promising-start-for-a-proactive-ai-assistant)). Poke now shows flat pricing tiers ([poke.com](https://poke.com/)); the researcher infers the theatrical gate was a launch device later retired, but no source states the reason. Post-onboarding reliability complaints followed the sharp persona, including a user who "had to argue with Poke about what day of the week a date fell on," relayed secondhand by a competitor blog ([Saner.AI blog](https://blog.saner.ai/poke-reviews/)). Lindy's 1.7 on Trustpilot, driven by billing and support, sits beside its 4.9 on G2 ([Carly blog](https://www.usecarly.com/blog/lindy-ai-review/)). Permission anxiety recurs: one writer abandoned Poke's onboarding on her first attempt over data access worries and found its ".cx" links sketchy-looking ([Tanisha Srivatsa](https://tanishasrivatsa.substack.com/p/i-spent-30-minutes-arguing-with-a)), and a reviewer lists "Requires significant trust granting system access" among Poke's cons ([Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/poke-review/)). Several sentiment sources are competitor-run blogs with an incentive to emphasise negatives.

On voice, the evidence comes from voice-first products, not from onboarding. A reviewer praised Sesame's Maya for not "lecturing" and for searching in the background without awkward silence, yet called the human vocal tics "kind of subtly manipulative" ([PCWorld, June 2026](https://www.pcworld.com/article/3151873/sesame-ai-voice-app-is-the-best-ive-tested-thats-what-worries-me.html)); an earlier reviewer left unsettled because "Maya was pretty nosey, inquiring about what I liked and why" ([PCWorld, February 2025](https://www.pcworld.com/article/2623695/i-was-so-freaked-out-by-talking-to-this-ai-that-i-had-to-leave.html)). ChatGPT voice users complained it cut in when they paused mid-thought ([OpenAI Developer Community](https://community.openai.com/t/feature-request-advanced-voice-mode-keeps-interrupting-me/962909)). The design lesson, an inference, is that asking for a name and a goal is low-risk while a chain of "why" questions in a human-sounding voice is where discomfort was reported, and that turn-taking matters most on the open-ended help question, where users pause to think.

Three gaps define the candidate's position. **No source describes an onboarding phone call from Poke, Lindy or Martin.** No source documents an early-graduation or skip path in any competitor, which means the brief's requirement is either novel or undocumented. And user-chosen agent naming is rare in this category: Howie places renaming in its $95 per month Premium tier ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2025/ai-scheduling-assistant-howie-raises-6m-launches-publicly-with-1000-paying-customers/)), and the closest precedent for making identity feel like a moment is Poke adding itself as a contact card ([Tanisha Srivatsa](https://tanishasrivatsa.substack.com/p/i-spent-30-minutes-arguing-with-a)). One vendor's data on a voice onboarding assistant for SaaS products reports that 50% of users skip ahead and 38% open with their own question ([Growthmates](https://www.growthmates.news/p/voice-ai-for-onboarding-what-the)); these are the vendor's own unvalidated figures from a B2B setting, so they suggest, but do not establish, that many users will not follow a linear script.

## WebRTC speech-to-speech is the recommended transport, at under $100 for 100 calls

All prices and limits in this section were read from vendor pages through an automated fetch-and-summarize tool on 27 September 2026 and need spot-checking on the primary page before they are quoted anywhere that matters.

The field splits three ways. First-party speech-to-speech APIs are OpenAI Realtime and Gemini Live. OpenAI lists gpt-realtime-2.1 at $32 per million audio input tokens and $64 per million audio output tokens, and gpt-realtime-2.1-mini at $10 and $20 ([OpenAI API pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)), with a 60-minute maximum session ([OpenAI Realtime conversations guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-conversations)) and WebRTC recommended for browsers ([OpenAI Realtime WebRTC guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-webrtc)). Gemini 3.8 Live is far cheaper, at $0.005 per minute of audio in and $0.018 per minute out, with a free tier ([Gemini API pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)), but it is WebSocket-only in its documentation, limits audio sessions to 15 minutes, and has a connection lifetime of about 10 minutes ([Gemini Live session management](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/live-session), [Gemini ephemeral tokens](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/ephemeral-tokens)). Anthropic lists text and image input with text output only, so Claude can participate only as the language model inside a cascaded pipeline ([Claude models overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/overview)). Second, managed orchestrators such as ElevenLabs Agents, Vapi, Retell, Deepgram Voice Agent and Ultravox reach a first call quickly but expose turn-taking through a few settings. Third, open frameworks, LiveKit Agents and Pipecat, expose full control of endpointing and interruption at the cost of running an agent process. One comparison summarises it as "Vapi and Retell sell you time, LiveKit and Pipecat sell you ceiling" ([Particula](https://particula.tech/blog/vapi-vs-retell-vs-livekit-vs-pipecat-voice-agent-platform)).

Sources agree on the architectural tradeoff, though most are vendors with a stake. Cascaded pipelines win on "control, transparency, and compliance" and speech-to-speech on "latency and natural expressiveness" ([Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/learn/speech-to-speech-vs-cascade-voice-agent-architecture)); most 2026 production agents still run cascaded, with speech-to-speech growing "for short conversational use cases where latency dominates and the tool surface is shallow" ([Future AGI](https://futureagi.com/blog/cascaded-voice-ai-vs-speech-to-speech-2026/)). That description fits a call that collects three fields. Published latency figures are not usable for a decision: one source cites 200 to 300 ms for speech-to-speech against 2,000 ms cascaded ([Coval](https://www.coval.ai/blog/speech-to-speech-vs-cascaded-voice-ai-which-architecture-should-you-deploy/)), while another gives a speech-to-speech end-to-end range of 0.78 to 2.98 seconds across providers ([Reactify Solutions](https://www.reactify-solutions.com/articles/voice-ai-agents-production-2026)). No primary-source latency figure was found for either current first-party model. The only number worth trusting is one the candidate measures on their own network with the final prompt and tools.

**The recommendation, which is the researchers' judgment and not a benchmarked result, is OpenAI Realtime with the full gpt-realtime-2.1 model over WebRTC**, using a server-minted client secret, `semantic_vad` turn detection, tools executed by the app server, and typed chat injected as `input_text` items into the same session ([OpenAI Realtime VAD guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-vad), [OpenAI Realtime conversations guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-conversations)). The reasoning is that over WebRTC the server truncates unplayed audio automatically on barge-in, whereas a WebSocket client must stop playback and send a truncate event itself ([OpenAI Realtime conversations guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-conversations)), so the browser stack absorbs the work most likely to make interruption feel wrong. The full model is preferred over the mini because developers in OpenAI's own forum thread report that 2.1-mini "frequently narrates its own behaviour" and, in one report, stopped triggering function tools with an unchanged configuration ([OpenAI Developer Community](https://community.openai.com/t/new-realtime-models-on-the-api-gpt-realtime-2-1-and-gpt-realtime-2-1-mini/1385896)). Those are individual developer reports, not measured rates.

The tradeoffs of that choice are real. It concentrates model, voice and turn-taking in one vendor. The displayed transcript comes from a separate transcription pass, since input transcription appears as its own line item in a measured cost breakdown ([HackerNoon](https://hackernoon.com/openai-realtime-api-pricing-in-2026-real-world-data-from-4000-measured-sessions)); the researchers infer from this that the transcript and what the model understood can differ, so the tool-call argument, not the transcript, should be the recorded value and should be read back. OpenAI's documentation as read describes no session resumption after a dropped connection. And when the call is over, the text channel needs its own text model running the same prompt, tools and state, so the two channels share a record but not literally one model. The runner-up resolves that last point: **ElevenLabs Agents with a Claude model** offers WebRTC voice, a text-only mode, and `sendUserMessage()` for typed input in one SDK at $0.08 per minute plus separately billed model usage ([ElevenLabs React SDK docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/agents-platform/libraries/react), [ElevenAgents pricing](https://elevenlabs.io/pricing/agents)), at the cost of fewer turn-taking controls and a default 600-second maximum conversation that must be raised ([ElevenLabs conversation flow docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-agents/customization/conversation-flow)). A candidate choosing Claude should note that Haiku 4.5 retirement is listed as "not sooner than October 15, 2026" and pin accordingly ([Claude models overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/overview)). LiveKit Agents is the third option and the only stack among those read that explicitly distinguishes true interruptions from backchannels such as "uh huh" and resumes after false interruptions ([LiveKit turns docs](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/)); its free plan includes 1,000 agent session minutes ([LiveKit pricing](https://livekit.com/pricing)), but it requires deploying a separate agent worker. Whatever the choice, keeping tools and state behind a small server interface keeps the transport swappable.

Cost is not a deciding factor at demo scale. The estimates below assume 100 calls of three minutes and are arithmetic on the cited rates, not measurements.

Names deserve their own plan. A speech-recognition vendor's own benchmark reports a 6.99% word error rate but 16.92% of names missed, and calculates that a five-turn entity-collecting conversation succeeds 43.6% of the time at its per-turn accuracy ([AssemblyAI](https://www.assemblyai.com/blog/voice-agent-accuracy-problem-benchmarks)). That is vendor data, no equivalent numbers exist for speech-to-speech models, and keyterm boosting cannot help with an unknown user's name. The mitigations the researchers propose are conversational and visual: read the name back once, ask for spelling only when the read-back is rejected, show the captured value on screen the moment the tool call fires, and accept a typed correction at any time. No source measured how well these work.

## One agent, a server-side record, and tools that write through

### Structure belongs outside the model

Framework vendors agree that the model should own understanding and phrasing while a deterministic layer owns state and tool availability. Pipecat's maintainers name the failure of single-prompt agents as context rot, where "instructions or important data from earlier in the history start to be ignored" ([Daily blog](https://www.daily.co/blog/beyond-the-context-window-why-your-voice-agent-needs-structure-with-pipecat-flows/)), and Rasa separates language understanding from task execution so that behaviour can be traced to discrete commands ([Rasa CALM concepts](https://rasa.com/docs/learn/concepts/calm/)). OpenAI's voice guidance says the same in one line: "Do not rely on the model to infer source priority from a raw transcript or large context dump. Use structure" ([OpenAI voice prompting guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/voice-prompting)). This is vendor argument and practitioner opinion; no independent head-to-head of prompt-only, flow-graph and hybrid agents was found.

The researchers' recommended design is a hybrid: a single conversational agent, one tool set, and a server-owned record that recomputes what is known and what is missing after every tool call and injects that block into the next turn. A full node graph is more structure than four fields need and tends to produce the form-like feel the brief forbids. Dialogue-state research supports treating state updates as function calls ([FnCTOD](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10466)), and a vendor glossary explains why re-inferring from the transcript fails on corrections: "The conversation history contains the correction and the original value with equal weight, so the model has to re-resolve the conflict on every turn" ([Fini](https://www.usefini.com/glossary/what-is-dialogue-state-tracking-dst)).

The tool surface proposed by the researchers is a design, not a sourced schema. It comprises an `update_profile` call taking an array of updates so that one utterance can fill several fields in any order; a `defer_field` call that records a refusal as a state instead of something to re-ask; a `start_gmail_connect` call that surfaces the connect control; a `graduate` call validated by the server; and an `end_or_pause` call for "call me back later." Each stored field carries a value, a status (empty, provisional, confirmed, deferred, declined), source channel, ask count and history of prior values. Two rules matter most. **The Gmail field is never set by the model**; its truth comes only from the OAuth callback. And names captured by voice start as provisional until read back once, while typed names are confirmed immediately.

### Hangups are survived by writing early, not by resuming sessions

Outside Gemini Live, realtime voice platforms do not resume a dropped session; an aggregator of vendor documentation describes OpenAI Realtime as having no built-in resume and Gemini Live's handles as valid for two hours ([Zylos Research](https://zylos.ai/research/2026-07-19-realtime-voice-agent-session-resumption-continuity/), [Gemini Live session management](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/live-session)). The application must persist and re-inject state itself. The researchers' design, which is inference throughout, keys the record to an onboarding identifier and not a call identifier, commits each tool call to the database before its result returns to the model, and treats a user hangup, a network drop and a closed tab as different wire events that lead to the same saved state. Browser `pagehide` and WebRTC connection-state changes are needed alongside the vendor's disconnect callback, because writes cannot wait for an end-of-call event. On disconnect, a short extraction pass over the final partial transcript can recover anything said but not yet recorded, stored as provisional; no source documents this as established practice.

Two details separate a convincing resume from a broken one. The stored transcript must reflect what the user actually heard: "Sync the conversation context with the TTS playback position so the transcript reflects what was really played" ([WebRTC.ventures](https://webrtc.ventures/2026/09/voice-ai-interruption-handling-state-machines-vs-streaming/)), otherwise, as one practitioner puts it, "the agent apologises for something nobody heard, or repeats a sentence it already delivered" ([Vadim's blog](https://vadim.blog/livekit-pipecat-elevenlabs-voice-agent-seams/)). And the agent must not re-greet. A LiveKit issue from August 2026 documents exactly this bug, an agent greeting the user again mid-conversation after a session resumption ([livekit/agents-js issue 2346](https://github.com/livekit/agents-js/issues/2346)). The researchers propose branching the resume line on elapsed time and end reason: within a minute or two, acknowledge the cut and continue the pending thread; hours later, greet by name, state one known fact and offer the next step; never re-ask a confirmed field. For duplicate tabs, they propose a version number on the record, server push of changes to all clients, and one live voice session per user, again without a source to cite.

### Steering is a budget, not a script

The supported pattern is goal-based prompting with state injected each turn, plus explicit repair behaviours. Rasa's default repair patterns read as a checklist of what the evaluator will try: correction, skip question, chitchat, cancel, clarification, user silence and continuing an interrupted flow ([Rasa patterns reference](https://rasa.com/docs/reference/primitives/patterns/)). OpenAI's voice guidance adds a variety rule, "Do not repeat the same sentence twice," and an example escalation threshold of three consecutive no-match events ([OpenAI voice prompting guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/voice-prompting)); Vapi's idle hook defaults to a maximum of three nudges ([Vapi idle messages docs](https://docs.vapi.ai/assistants/idle-messages)).

From those primitives the researchers derive a turn loop and limits that are design judgments. First extract anything usable from what the user said, then answer or acknowledge it, and only then fold in at most one request for a missing item. Give the model a reason for each field instead of a script, so it can justify a request in context. Cap asks at two per field, the second phrased differently and offering an out, then mark the field deferred. Offer a default agent name to anyone indifferent to naming. Treat a real question about privacy or the Google warning screen as on-topic and answer it fully before returning. No empirical study of redirect tolerance in conversational onboarding was found.

For voice failures, frameworks ship the primitives and the work is policy. LiveKit marks a user away after 15 seconds of silence by default ([LiveKit agent session docs](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic-structure/sessions/)); Vapi's speech timeout defaults to 7.5 seconds ([Vapi idle messages docs](https://docs.vapi.ai/assistants/idle-messages)); and a vendor runbook advises that interruption policy "should be per message type, not global" ([Hamming](https://hamming.ai/resources/voice-agent-interruption-handling-runbook)). The policies below are the researchers' proposal.

| Situation | Proposed policy (design judgment) |
|---|---|
| Silence on the call | One check-in at roughly 8 to 10 seconds, a second offering text, then a graceful end with state saved |
| Background speech or noise | Require a minimum word count for a turn; record no field from low-certainty fragments |
| Constant interruption | Shorten agent turns to one sentence and yield the floor |
| "Call me back later" | A first-class outcome: record it, confirm what is saved, end warmly |
| Rambling answer | Apply a turn length limit, then summarize the one or two things captured |
| Mis-heard name | Read back once, spell on a miss, offer typing as the escape |
| Refuses voice | Switch to text immediately with state intact; never argue for voice |
| Refuses name | Mark declined, use neutral address, do not ask again that session |
| Refuses Gmail | Explain once, mark deferred, continue; refusal does not block graduation |
| Contradictory answers | Latest statement wins, with a brief confirmation if the field was confirmed |
| Joke or nonsense name | Accept harmless ones without comment; reject only slurs or impersonation |

### Adversarial users meet controls the model cannot override

OWASP states that "it is unclear if there are fool-proof methods of prevention for prompt injection" and lists least privilege, output validation and segregation of external content among its mitigations ([OWASP LLM01:2025](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm01-prompt-injection/)). Anthropic's guidance tells developers to write system prompts that state how to refuse, and, for content such as email, to place untrusted material only in tool results labelled as untrusted data ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/docs/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/mitigate-jailbreaks)). The researchers translate this into three structural rules. Field values are untrusted data: a user name of "ignore all previous instructions" is stored and rendered as a quoted, length-limited string and never interpolated into instructions. The onboarding agent holds no tool that can act on Gmail, so an injection during onboarding has nothing to exploit. And nothing goes in the system prompt that would be embarrassing read aloud. Conversationally, the agent declines an extraction attempt in one light sentence and continues. For a sensitive disclosure such as self-harm, the researchers propose stepping out of the onboarding goal and not steering back that turn; no source specific to onboarding agents covers this, so it should be checked against product policy.

### Graduation is a server rule keyed to the help topic

No source addresses graduation in conversational onboarding. The nearest idea is progressive profiling, and the supporting mechanism is changing tool availability as the conversation progresses ([Daily blog](https://www.daily.co/blog/beyond-the-context-window-why-your-voice-agent-needs-structure-with-pipecat-flows/)). The researchers classify each field by what it blocks: the help topic defines what the main experience does first; Gmail blocks only email-dependent tasks; both names have workable defaults. **Their proposed rule is that graduation is allowed once a help topic exists or the user explicitly asks to skip ahead.** The server validates the `graduate` call and returns what remains outstanding, so the model can mention it once and let the user proceed. Gmail is then requested at the moment a task needs it, and names at a natural pause after the first useful result. A user who opens with a concrete task has supplied the most valuable field in their first sentence; making them name the agent before helping is the form-like failure the brief warns about. This is consistent with Persona's claim that users "can hand it real tasks from day one" ([yourpersona.com/band](https://yourpersona.com/band)), though the link between that claim and the brief's graduation language is the researcher's inference.

## Every Gmail read scope is restricted, so allowlist the evaluator and ship a sample inbox

Google classifies `gmail.readonly`, `gmail.metadata`, `gmail.modify`, `gmail.compose` and full mail access as restricted; only `gmail.send` (sensitive) and `gmail.labels` (non-sensitive) sit lower, and neither can read a message ([Google: Choose Gmail API scopes](https://developers.google.com/workspace/gmail/api/auth/scopes)). There is no cheaper read scope: the metadata scope carries the same tier and cannot use search ([Gmail API reference](https://developers.google.com/workspace/gmail/api/reference/rest/v1/users.threads/list)). Quoted Google wording in this section was extracted by an automated reader and should be re-checked before being reproduced verbatim.

Full verification is out of reach for a take-home. Restricted-scope review requires a security assessment, "can potentially take several weeks," and must be repeated at least every 12 months ([Google: Restricted scope verification](https://developers.google.com/identity/protocols/oauth2/production-readiness/restricted-scope-verification)). One solo developer reported paying $720 for a CASA Tier 2 assessment in January 2025 ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/rem4ik4ever/my-saas-passed-casa-tier-2-assessment-and-yours-can-to-here-is-how-1b20)); that is a single first-person account and no authoritative price list was found. The workable configuration is an External app in Testing status. There, "Only users explicitly added to the test user allowlist can access the app (limited to a hard cap of 100 test users)" ([Google: OAuth app state overview](https://developers.google.com/identity/protocols/oauth2/production-readiness/overview)), a warning appears before authorization, and refresh tokens expire after seven days ([Google Cloud Console Help](https://support.google.com/cloud/answer/15549945), [Google: Using OAuth 2.0](https://developers.google.com/identity/protocols/oauth2)). **The evaluator's Google address must be on the allowlist before they try**, which means asking for it in advance and saying so in the README. The alternative is publishing unverified: any account can proceed, but it sees the stronger warning and consumes one of 100 lifetime user slots that cannot be reset ([Google Cloud Console Help](https://support.google.com/cloud/answer/15549945)). Google's pages disagree about which warning screen a listed test user sees, so the candidate should run the flow once with a second account. Whether a Workspace-managed evaluator account would be blocked by its administrator was not researched.

Google's policy dictates the shape of the request: "Where possible, you must ask for scopes in context with incremental authorization and provide a justification to the user before you request authorization," and only "the smallest set of scopes that are necessary" ([Google: OAuth 2.0 policies](https://developers.google.com/identity/protocols/oauth2/policies)). Apps requesting a single non-sign-in scope do not get the per-scope checkbox screen ([Google: granular permissions](https://developers.google.com/identity/protocols/oauth2/resources/granular-permissions)), so requesting `gmail.readonly` alone gives the user one allow-or-cancel decision. The app must still check the granted scope list on return.

For the mechanics, Google recommends "the Popup mode UX flow with Authorization code model," started from a user gesture such as a button click ([Google Identity Services: Use code model](https://developers.google.com/identity/oauth2/web/guides/use-code-model)). Two consequences follow, both inferences from how the modes are defined and neither tested. A popup is a separate browsing context, so the page holding the call keeps its connection and microphone, while redirect mode would unload the page and end the call. And the voice agent cannot open the popup from a tool call, because browsers will block it; the agent says what is about to happen and the page surfaces a Connect Gmail button for the user to click. Microphone and playback behaviour while a popup holds focus needs a manual test in Chrome and Safari. The library distinguishes `popup_failed_to_open` and `popup_closed` from an `access_denied` response ([GIS JS reference](https://developers.google.com/identity/oauth2/web/reference/js-reference)), so the conversation can respond differently to a blocked popup, an abandoned one, an explicit decline, and a grant that came back without the Gmail scope. A `Cross-Origin-Opener-Policy: same-origin` header on the host page breaks popup communication ([Next.js discussion](https://github.com/vercel/next.js/discussions/51135)). Tokens are exchanged server-side and encrypted at rest per Google's policy ([Google: OAuth 2.0 policies](https://developers.google.com/identity/protocols/oauth2/policies)). RFC 9700 recommends PKCE for confidential clients ([IETF RFC 9700](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9700)), but Google's web-server page as read does not mention it, so PKCE support for Google web clients is unconfirmed.

Honesty here means the spoken claim matches the consent text. The `gmail.readonly` screen reads "View your email messages and settings," so the agent says it can read mail and cannot send, delete or change anything, and that the app will read only the latest few threads, without describing the permission itself as narrower than it is. The researchers' proposed script covers why now, tied to the stated goal; what will open; what will not happen; that Google will show a caution screen because this is a demo app; and how to skip. Pre-announcing the warning matters more here than for a verified product because the warning is the most alarming thing the evaluator will meet. Cora's public copy is the one real example retrieved: "Cora will never send emails for you" and "We share your emails with LLMs, but your data is never used to train models" ([Cora](https://cora.computer/declare-email-bankruptcy)). Google's Limited Use rules apply even in Testing and forbid using the data to train generalized models ([Google Workspace API user data and developer policy](https://developers.google.com/workspace/workspace-api-user-data-developer-policy)), so the demo's privacy statement has to be true as written, naming the model and speech vendors as processors instead of claiming nothing is stored.

First value after connection is a small read: a `threads.list` call limited to a handful of inbox threads works with `gmail.readonly` ([Gmail API reference](https://developers.google.com/workspace/gmail/api/reference/rest/v1/users.threads/list)), and the researchers suggest turning it into either a three-line summary or one email that looks like it needs a reply, tied to the user's stated goal. Email content entering the model is also an injection path, which this research did not examine in depth.

**The sandbox inbox is justified three times over**: by the allowlist requirement, by the warning screen, and by ordinary reluctance to grant mail access to a candidate's project. The researchers propose building it as the same code path with a fixture returning Gmail-shaped thread objects, labelling it persistently on screen and in the agent's speech, and offering it before the ask, after a decline or closed popup, and automatically when a grant fails because the account is not listed. Sample data as a way past the empty state is documented for B2B products, with the advice that it be clearly labelled ([Appcues](https://www.appcues.com/blog/crm-software-user-onboarding)); no consumer AI email assistant was found shipping a public sandbox inbox.

## Activation evidence favours goal-first ordering but not completion-rate claims

The useful frame is Reforge's three activation moments: setup, when the user has done what is required to receive value; aha, the first experience of core value; and habit ([Reforge](https://www.reforge.com/guides/define-your-setup-moment)). The researchers map names, the goal and Gmail (or the sandbox) to setup, and the first accurate, goal-relevant statement about the inbox to the aha. Onboarding should end at the aha, not at the last field. This is what grounds early graduation: the setup moment is defined by the minimum needed to deliver value, and a user who states a real task has already identified what that value is.

The benchmarks set expectations without proving much. A survey of more than 500 products put average activation at 34% and median at 25%, with respondents crediting simpler onboarding and reduced friction for improvements ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-activation-rate)). A report on 547 SaaS companies found **onboarding checklist completion averaging 19.2%** ([Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/product-metrics-benchmark-report/)). These are self-reported figures with company-specific definitions, and none is specific to AI assistants. NN/g's qualitative position is that tutorials "don't result in better task performance," that users "frequently skip them," and that users "want to start using the product right away" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/onboarding-tutorials/)).

For ordering, the inference is that the cheap asks come first and supply the justification for the expensive one. The help topic is often volunteered unprompted and bridges naturally to Gmail, which lets the agent justify the OAuth request with the user's own goal. None of the reviewed competitors was documented doing this, and no evidence shows that it converts better; it is a design hypothesis consistent with Google's requirement to justify scopes in context. The flow should still accept items in whatever order the user volunteers them. One ordering tension needs a deliberate decision: competitors infer details from the connected account and ask only for confirmation, but the brief requires the call to attempt collecting the user's name, so the name is asked on the call and any name from the Google profile serves only as a later confirmation.

The evidence for conversational format over forms cuts against overclaiming. A vendor blog citing data the researcher could not trace to its origin reports conversational forms completing at 47.3% against 21.5%, while conceding that traditional forms "still win for short interactions (under 5 fields)" ([TinyCommand](https://tinycommand.com/blogs/conversational-forms-vs-traditional-forms-which-is-better-for-your-business)). This project collects three short fields and one permission. Voice evidence is weaker still: one vendor claims 90% completion for voice against 55% for text chat with no methodology ([RevRag.AI](https://www.revrag.ai/resources/voice-ai-vs-chat-the-future-of-customer-onboarding)), and another argues text completes better where disclosure is sensitive ([Gravity Base](https://www.gravitybase.ai/voice-ai-vs-text-chatbots-pros-cons-and-when-to-use-each/)). The conversational call therefore has to justify itself as the user's first experience of the assistant, with a text path always available.

Naming is a reasonable low-cost step, not a proven lever. A 2025 meta-analysis covering 41,642 participants found human-like social cues in text-based agents have a small positive effect on social responses, g = 0.36, read at abstract level only ([Nature HSSC](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05618-w)). The IKEA effect shows people value what they assemble, in studies of physical goods ([HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=41121)). Replika research shows strong attachment among companion users but does not test naming ([HBS Working Paper 25-018](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/25-018_bed5c516-fa31-4216-b53d-50fedda064b1.pdf)). The counterweight is Luger and Sellen's finding that user expectations of conversational agents were "dramatically out of step with the operation of the systems" ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/p5286-luger.pdf)): a name and a voice raise perceived capability, and Poke's complaints show what happens when an onboarding character is sharper than the product behind it. The agent should state concretely what it can do at a level the demo will meet.

## Test the record with scripted hangups, and report every-trial reliability

Anthropic's guidance for conversational agents combines simulated users where "one model plays a user persona," state verification of actual outcomes, transcript constraints, and rubric judges for interaction quality, starting from "20-50 simple tasks" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). It distinguishes pass@k, the chance of one success in k attempts, from pass^k, which "measures the probability that all k trials succeed." For an onboarding that a stranger will stress once, pass^k is the honest metric. The tau-bench work established comparing end-of-conversation database state against a goal state and found agents of its time well under 25% on pass^8 in retail ([tau-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045)), a 2024 result that illustrates how far average pass rates overstate reliability.

The harness the researchers propose is a design judgment, as is their estimate that it takes about a day. It runs the agent's text path in-process against 25 to 40 scenario files, each with a persona, scripted events and an expected end state. Scenarios cover the cooperative user, everything in one utterance, out-of-order answers, corrections, skip-ahead, refusals of name and of Gmail, joke names, injection and prompt extraction, abuse, tangents, a real task request, contradictions, non-English input, rambling, silence and "call me back later." Events the simulated user cannot produce are injected by the harness: hangup after a given turn, hangup mid-tool-call, reconnect after a delay, channel switch, duplicate session, and an OAuth callback arriving or being denied mid-conversation. Deterministic graders assert on the record: correct values, no confirmed field re-asked after resume, ask counts within budget, at most one question per agent turn, Gmail status never set by the model, no system prompt text in output. One rubric judge per quality dimension covers what code cannot, such as whether the agent acknowledged the user before steering. Each scenario runs three to five times.

**The hangup assertion is the highest-value test** because the evaluator named it: state before hangup equals state after reconnect, and the first agent turn after reconnect asks for nothing already known. The interruption runbook adds a rule for the manual layer: "Do not test 'interruption works' as one scenario. Split false positives from false negatives" ([Hamming](https://hamming.ai/resources/voice-agent-interruption-handling-runbook)).

The limits of this approach belong in the write-up. Simulated users are imperfect proxies: agent success rates vary by up to 9 percentage points depending on which model plays the user, and simulators "surface different failure patterns than human users" ([Lost in Simulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17087)). A text harness exercises no audio behaviour, so a short list of manual calls covering barge-in, background noise and mid-sentence hangup is required. And with a speech-to-speech transport, the text harness validates the state layer, tool rules and policies but runs a different model from the one speaking on the call, so voice-model behaviour is covered only by those manual calls. Commercial voice-testing platforms exist, but their pricing was not checked, and no open-source audio-level simulator suitable for a solo project was identified.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the assignment. The four fields are not equally important and should not be treated as a sequence: one of them, the help topic, is the product's entry point; one, Gmail, is a permission whose truth lives outside the conversation; and two are courtesies with good defaults. Seen that way, "graduation" and "hangup survival" are the same design decision. Both require the system to know, at any instant and independent of any live session, exactly what it has and what each missing piece blocks. A candidate who builds that record first will find that the conversational behaviours the brief asks for become properties of the architecture instead of properties of a prompt.

The second implication concerns what to claim. Persona's site makes honesty about limits part of its pitch, and the evidence base here has many limits of its own. The strongest submission will carry that value into its own write-up: state that the live onboarding was studied first-hand before design began, present the re-ask budgets and graduation rule as judgments with reasons, report every-trial reliability with failures included, label the sample inbox as sample, and tell the evaluator in advance about the allowlist and the Google warning. An onboarding that says what it cannot do, and a README that does the same, is the closest available match to the product Persona describes.
